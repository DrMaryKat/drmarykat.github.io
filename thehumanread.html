00

OrientationThe premise, and your problem of practice

Human-centered research is a qualitative approach, used in design and increasingly in organizational leadership, for understanding people's needs, behaviors, motivations, and experiences. It uses interviews, observation, and analysis of real-world interaction to find patterns in how people think and act, then translates those patterns into better decisions, programs, policies, and practices. For an educational leader, those people are teachers, students, families, staff, and the communities a school or system serves, and the better decisions are the ones a leader is actually positioned to make.

Artificial intelligence does not change that goal. What it changes is your reach. AI can read a hundred transcripts in the time you read one, hold a dozen competing framings in view at once, and play a participant back to you before you have spoken to a single real person. Used carelessly, that same speed flattens nuance, manufactures false confidence, and substitutes a model's fluent average for a person's specific truth. This course is built around a single discipline: let AI accelerate the work that scales, and protect for yourself the work that should not.

◆ A bridge for the doctoral reader

The studio metaphors that title these lessons (lens, frame, aperture, focus) are a scaffold, not the substance. Each ties back to the methodology a leader-scholar already answers to: reflexivity, problem framing, purposive sampling, instrument validity, thematic analysis, and member checking. The work stays anchored in a real leadership problem of practice, set in the schools, districts, or policy contexts you serve. If you teach inquiry in educational leadership, those bridges are yours to lift into a syllabus.

Name your leadership problem of practice

Everything you build in this course will orbit one leadership problem of practice: a persistent, specific, and contextualized issue embedded in the educational organization you lead or serve, the kind of problem a leader is positioned to investigate and act on. A workable problem of practice has four properties: it is grounded in a context you actually work in or have access to; it affects real people with something at stake; you hold genuine uncertainty about it (you cannot already write the findings); and it is bounded enough to study, yet consequential enough to matter to the system. State it as an open question, not a foregone conclusion, and not as a solution looking for permission.

Too broad
"How do we improve school leadership?" Not situated in any context you can act on.
Grounded PoP
"How do early-career teachers in our building experience instructional feedback from leadership, and what makes it feel supportive versus evaluative?"
Solution in disguise
"How do we get teachers to adopt the new walkthrough tool?" The remedy is assumed before the problem is understood.
Grounded PoP
"What do families of multilingual learners in our district need from school leaders to feel like genuine partners in decision-making?"
Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
The running problem of practice

"What do families of multilingual learners in our district need from school leaders to feel like genuine partners in decision-making?"

The brief

A suburban district where multilingual enrollment has risen roughly 40 percent in five years. Family participation in the bodies where decisions actually get made (governance councils, the Title III parent advisory) stays persistently low. Leaders assume language access is the barrier but have never tested that assumption. Affected: multilingual families, building principals, the district MLL office. Genuinely uncertain: whether the gap is language, trust, scheduling, prior experience, or something no one has named. Why now: the district is rewriting its family-engagement plan this year. We will follow this one problem through every lesson that follows.

01

Interrogate Your Lens (Reflexivity)Surface your assumptions before you study anyone

Every researcher looks through a lens ground by their own history, training, and position. That lens decides what you notice and, more dangerously, what you never think to look for. In the academic tradition this is reflexivity: the disciplined examination of how the researcher shapes the research. Before you point any instrument at a participant, you turn it on yourself.

AI gives you two unusual mirrors. First, you can study the model itself as a kind of cultural informant, asking what worldview its training encodes and where it skews. Second, you can use it as a tireless Socratic partner that keeps asking what you are assuming until you can see the shape of your own lens.

Move one: interrogate the AI ethnographically

Treat the model the way an ethnographer treats an informant who comes from a particular place. It does not hold a neutral view of your topic; it holds a fluent average of its training data, which over-represents some voices and silences others. Name that before you trust anything it tells you about people.

Prompt · model-as-informant
You are a research informant, not an authority. My problem of practice is:
"[your problem of practice]".

1. When you describe the people affected by this problem, whose
   experiences are you most likely drawing on, and whose are likely thin
   or missing in what you were trained on?
2. What assumptions about [the population] tend to show up as if they
   were neutral facts?
3. Where might your fluency make a stereotype sound like a finding?
Answer plainly. Do not reassure me.

Move two: let the AI interrogate you

Now flip the chair. Give the model permission to press on your certainties. The goal is not answers; it is the surfacing of premises you did not know you held.

Prompt · the Socratic mirror
Interview me about my problem of practice as a skeptical methodologist.
Ask one question at a time. After each answer, name the assumption
embedded in what I just said before asking the next question.
Keep going until you have surfaced at least six assumptions I am
treating as facts. Then steelman a framing of this problem that
is the opposite of mine.
⚠ Ethical guardrail

The model's fluency is not representativeness. Its confident description of a community is a reflection of dominant-culture training data, not testimony from that community. Use this lesson to find your blind spots, never to fill them with synthetic understanding. The remedy for not knowing a population is talking to them, not asking a model to imagine them.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
What the lens surfaced
  • I treated language access as the barrier; it may be one of several, or a symptom of something else.
  • I equated partnership with attendance at our meetings, on our schedule, in our format.
  • I assumed families want the involvement structures we already offer.
  • My own monolingual schooling shapes what "engagement" even looks like to me.
Caution from the model-as-informant probe

Asked to describe these families, the model produced broad, faintly deficit-tinged generalizations drawn from dominant narratives about immigrant parents. Useful as a mirror of the bias to avoid; useless as testimony about anyone real. Logged in the reflexivity memo.

02

Compose Your Frame (Problem Framing)How you pose the problem decides what you can find

A research question is a lens cut to a particular shape. Frame the problem as a solution waiting for adoption and you will only ever find adoption barriers. Frame it as an open human question and the whole problem space opens. The work of this lesson is to deconstruct your problem of practice, separate the problem space from the solution space, and compose a frame generative enough to surprise you.

Deconstruct, then reframe

Use the model to pull your problem of practice apart and rebuild it several ways. Two classic moves translate cleanly into prompts: abstraction laddering (asking "why" to move up toward purpose, "how" to move down toward tactics) and How Might We reframing, which converts a problem into an invitation without smuggling in the answer.

Prompt · reframe the problem
Here is my problem of practice: "[problem of practice]".

a) Build an abstraction ladder: ask "why does this matter" three times
   to climb toward purpose, then "how might it happen" three times to
   descend toward the concrete.
b) Generate five distinct "How Might We" reframings at different altitudes
   on that ladder. None may contain a baked-in solution.
c) For each, name what it would let me see that my original framing hides,
   and what it would cause me to miss.
◆ Methodological cousin

This is the construction of your research problem and questions, the move that anchors a proposal's first chapter. The reframings are candidate research questions; choosing among them is a positioning argument, not a clerical task.

⚠ Ethical guardrail

Models optimize for the plausible, which is to say the conventional. Left alone, AI will anchor you to the most common framing of your topic, the one most represented in its data. Treat its first frame as the baseline to push against, not the destination. Novelty is your contribution; the model supplies the average.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
Abstraction ladder + reframings
  • Original: "What do families need to feel like partners?"
  • Up (why it matters): "How might we share real decision-making power with communities the system has historically sidelined?"
  • Down (how it happens): "How might leaders redesign the specific moments where decisions get made so families can shape them?"
  • Lateral: "How might we learn what partnership already looks like inside these families' own networks, and meet it there?"
Frame selected, and why

The lateral frame. It refuses the deficit premise that families lack engagement and instead asks what engagement already exists that the district fails to see. Deliberately set aside for now: the formal-governance angle, which can be revisited once we understand what partnership means to families on their own terms.

03

Widen Your Aperture (Purposive Sampling)Plan for range, not for the average

A camera's aperture controls how much light enters. A research plan's aperture controls how much of human variety you let in. Most weak plans recruit the convenient and the typical, then discover only the typical. A strong plan samples for range: it deliberately seeks edge cases, extreme users, and contexts where the phenomenon strains. In academic terms you are doing purposive, maximum-variation sampling rather than recruiting whoever answers first.

Brainstorm wide, then check who is missing

AI is genuinely strong at fast, divergent enumeration. Use it to generate far more participant types, contexts, and method options than you will use, then prune. Critically, follow every brainstorm with an equity check.

Prompt · widen, then audit
My frame is: "[chosen frame]".

1. Propose 12 distinct participant archetypes, spanning mainstream cases
   AND edge cases (extreme users, reluctant users, people the system
   currently fails). For each, note why their perspective would stretch
   my understanding.
2. List the contexts and moments where I should observe this phenomenon,
   not just ask about it.
3. Recommend a menu of methods (interview, observation, diary, artifact
   elicitation) matched to what each would reveal.
4. Now audit your own list: whose voice is conspicuously absent, and what
   would I have to do in the real world to reach them?
⚠ Ethical guardrail

The model's sense of "who is missing" is bounded by the same training that produced the gap. It will name the absences it has language for and stay silent on the ones it does not. Your lived and contextual knowledge has to extend the list. Beware extractive sampling, too: recruiting marginalized participants to mine their experience without reciprocity is a harm, not a method.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
Participant range (sampling for variation, not the average)
  • A newcomer family (under one year) and a long-settled family (ten-plus years)
  • A family whose child recently exited EL services
  • Families across at least three different home languages
  • Families who never attend anything, reached through a trusted liaison rather than a district email
  • A bilingual front-office staff member, the real daily touchpoint between families and the school
Contexts and who is missing

Observe where partnership actually happens (pickup, community and faith settings, home visits), not only the formal meeting. Hardest to reach and most important: families without reliable transport or childcare, and families wary of any institutional contact. The access plan routes through a community liaison, with reciprocity built in, rather than extracting time from families who already feel unseen.

04

Calibrate Your Focus (Instrument Design)Tune the instrument before you point it at people

A protocol full of leading or confusing questions corrupts data no matter how skilled the analysis that follows. Instrument quality is the cheapest place to improve a study and the most often skipped. Good qualitative questions are open, non-leading, and laddered from broad grand-tour prompts ("walk me through a typical week") down to specific probes. This lesson uses AI to rehearse and pressure-test your guide before you spend a real participant's time on it.

Run a simulated interview, then flip it

Have the model role-play one of your archetypes so you can hear how your questions land. Then reverse the roles: let the model interview you as a participant, which exposes leading phrasing and dead ends faster than reading the guide ever will.

Prompt · simulated participant
Role-play this participant archetype: "[archetype + 3 traits]".
Stay in character, give realistic, sometimes guarded answers, and do not
be more articulate or cooperative than a real person would be.
I will interview you with my guide below. After we finish, drop character
and tell me which of my questions were leading, confusing, double-barreled,
or answerable with one word, and rewrite the three weakest.

My guide:
[paste your questions]
Prompt · interview me
Now interview ME as if I were a participant in this study, using my guide.
Ask one question at a time and follow my answers with natural probes.
Afterward, tell me where the guide lost momentum, where I gave thin answers,
and where a better-sequenced question would have opened me up.
⚠ Ethical guardrail

A simulated participant is a rehearsal tool, never a data source. Synthetic answers are the model's prediction of what a person might say, drawn from its average, and they carry none of the truth, surprise, or accountability of a real interview. Use simulations to sharpen instruments. Never report them as findings, and never let them stand in for the people you owe real contact.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
What the rehearsal exposed

The simulated parent stalled on the question "How has the language barrier affected your involvement?" Two flaws surfaced at once: it presupposes language is the problem, and it presumes the family's involvement is low. The model, in character, gave a short, guarded answer; out of character, it named the embedded assumption.

Revised

Replaced with a grand-tour opener: "Walk me through the last time the school asked your opinion about a decision. What happened?" The changelog records the rationale: strip the embedded assumption, and let the family define what involvement means before the protocol does.

05

Expand Your Perspective (Thematic Analysis)Analyze qualitative data at scale, without losing the human read

This is where AI's scale earns its place. Once you hold transcripts, survey text, field notes, and other unstructured data, AI can perform a fast first pass across all of it at once, surfacing candidate codes, clustering them into themes, and flagging the negative cases that disconfirm your story. What it cannot do is interpret, because interpretation is the act of deciding what the patterns mean for real people, and that judgment is yours.

◆ Methodological cousin

This maps directly onto established thematic analysis: familiarization, generating initial codes, searching for and reviewing themes, defining them, and writing up (the Braun and Clarke arc). AI can accelerate the early coding labor; the reviewing and defining of themes is where your interpretive authority lives. A codebook with clear definitions and exemplar quotes (in the spirit of Saldaña's coding manual) is what keeps the AI's first pass honest.

Build a custom analyst, not a magic button

Rather than pasting transcripts into a fresh chat each time, build a reusable custom GPT or project with standing instructions, your codebook, and a few hand-coded exemplars. That turns the model into a consistent first-pass coder you supervise.

System instructions · custom qualitative analyst
ROLE: You are a first-pass qualitative coding assistant. You do not have
final say; a human researcher reviews everything you produce.

RULES:
- Code only what is in the text. Never invent or paraphrase a quote.
- Every code you assign must cite the exact verbatim line that supports it.
- When evidence is ambiguous, say so and offer competing readings.
- Flag negative cases that contradict an emerging theme. Do not smooth them.
- Report both frequency (how often) and salience (how strongly), and never
  let frequency stand in for importance.

CODEBOOK: [paste codes + definitions + exemplar quotes]
Prompt · run a coding pass
Here is a de-identified transcript. Apply the codebook.
Return a table: Code | Verbatim evidence (exact line) | Confidence | Notes.
Then list any passages that fit NO existing code and may need a new one.
Then name 2 negative cases that challenge the themes forming so far.
⚠ Ethical guardrail · this one is non-negotiable

De-identify before anything touches a model, and confirm your IRB protocol and the platform's data terms permit it. Participant data is a trust, not a convenience. Two further hazards: models fabricate quotes that look real, so verify every cited line against the source transcript; and a model will happily smooth a messy human into a tidy theme, so guard the contradictions and the outliers, because that is where the truth often hides. Where stakes are high, return themes to participants for member checking.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
First-pass themes (model-coded, human-reviewed)
  • Respect is read through small gestures: who greets them, who remembers their child's name
  • Decisions arrive feeling already made; the invitation to give "input" feels ceremonial
  • Trust attaches to individuals (a particular teacher, a liaison), not to "the school"
  • Scheduling is interpreted as a signal of whether the family is genuinely valued
Negative case and safeguards

One family does feel like a true partner. The difference is a single bilingual staff member who follows up personally; that outlier reframes the entire analysis and becomes the spine of the recommendation. All transcripts were de-identified before any AI pass, consistent with the IRB protocol and the platform's data terms, and the emerging themes were returned to three families for member checking before anything was reported.

06

Share Your Insight (Knowledge Mobilization)Turn findings into something that moves people

An insight that no one acts on is a private fact. Influence comes from translation: shaping the same finding into the form a given audience can receive. A provost needs a one-page provocation; a program or leadership team needs a persona and a journey map; a community needs to see itself represented with dignity. AI is a fast and flexible co-writer for trying many formats, drafting each so you can choose and then edit for truth and voice.

Generate formats, keep the authority

Prompt · multi-format treatment
Here are my three core findings, each with supporting verbatim evidence:
[findings + quotes].

Draft four short treatments of the SAME findings for four audiences:
1. A skeptical decision-maker (one-page provocation, leads with stakes).
2. A program or leadership team (persona + a simple journey map in words).
3. The participants themselves (a respectful "here is what we heard" note).
4. A scholarly audience (an abstract-style synthesis).
Keep every claim traceable to the evidence I gave you. Flag anywhere you
are tempted to generalize beyond it.
⚠ Ethical guardrail

A model writes to please, which means it will round corners, dramatize, and invent the satisfying detail. Every claim in your artifact must trace to evidence you actually gathered; if you cannot point to the line, cut it. Represent participants as they are, not as the story wants them. The polish is the model's gift; the truthfulness is your signature.

Studio Thread · Multilingual Family Partnership
The artifact

A one-page provocation for the superintendent's cabinet, timed to the family-engagement plan rewrite. It reframes the goal from "raise family attendance" to "redistribute where decisions get made," carried by a persona, Ms. Okafor, built from the composite evidence rather than any single quoted family, plus one journey map of how families currently experience a decision being made about their children.

The ask and the evidence trail

Requested action: pilot two community-sited decision points next semester, co-hosted with a trusted liaison. Every claim in the artifact traces back to a coded theme, and the lone partnership success story (the bilingual staff member who follows up) carries the recommendation. Nothing is dramatized beyond what the evidence supports.

07

Looking AheadMindsets are the tool that outlasts the tools

The specific models will change; some of the prompts in this course will be obsolete within a year. What endures is the posture you bring to the work. Four mindsets carry across whatever the technology becomes.

01

Reflexive

Know your lens. Treat the model as another lens with its own distortions, and name both before you trust either.

02

Generative

Use AI to widen, diverge, and reframe early, where wrong answers are cheap and range is the goal.

03

Skeptical

Verify every quote, guard every contradiction, and refuse fluency as evidence. Confidence is not accuracy.

04

Human-first

Protect the parts that should stay human: contact with real people, interpretation, and accountability for the story you tell.
